Procrastination Among College Students

A data story about putting things off, and why it's probably not just you.

Built on a synthetic, AI-generated dataset of 300 survey responses. The patterns we show are correlations, not proof of cause and effect.

You are not the only one: data shows that 47% of college students procrastinate.

Nieto-Fernández et al. (2024), International Journal of Instruction, 17(2).

We call ourselves procrastinators like it's a fixed trait.

We wanted to know if that's true, so we looked at students across every age and routine we could measure, and asked:

What else changes along with it?

Procrastination isn't constant.

The first thing we noticed: it changes with age. Younger participants scored higher, older ones consistently lower, a first hint that it isn't a fixed trait.

College students sit right in the highest-scoring group, so we looked at what else sets that age apart.

The countdown effect

Our first guess was deadlines: more time left should mean less procrastination.

The data says the opposite. Scores were lowest five to seven days out and climbed most in the final few days, which makes sense once you see what else happens in those days.

Too much, all at once.

Assignments, work and everything else pile up until even deciding where to start feels impossible.

Feeling overwhelmed had one of the strongest links to procrastination in the dataset. The most overwhelmed students reported the highest scores, and it matches the deadline pattern: students closer to a deadline also felt more overwhelmed.

Not just a willpower problem.

We also looked at something less obvious: sleep.

Students who slept less reported higher scores, one of the cleanest patterns in the dataset. Being tired isn't a character flaw, yet in our data it's linked to procrastination about as strongly as the psychological factors.

The one thing you can actually control.

Age, deadlines, overwhelm and sleep are hard to change in the moment. Planning is something you can start today.

Of 51 students, those with no planning system averaged 4.55 out of 5; those who planned in any way, even in their heads, averaged 3.91. All 22 non-planners scored 4.3 or higher, against 3 of the 29 planners.

That's the turning point: this one you can act on, though our data can't show planning alone lowers procrastination.

Synthesis

Overwhelm, sleep and planning were the three factors most consistently linked to procrastination, more than age, deadlines, gender or occupation.

None of them are fixed, even though "I'm just a procrastinator" assumes they are.

What to look for: at ages 18–24, students with no plan score highest (4.55 vs 3.76 for reactive or deadline-driven planners).

What you can try this week.

One small experiment per factor. Our data shows links, not proof, so test them on yourself.

Data

Procrastination Habits and Behaviors dataset on Kaggle: 300 respondents, 68 attributes.

A note on the data

The dataset is synthetic and AI-generated, so some relationships are stronger than you'd expect in real life. Read the patterns as prompts for reflection, not as proof.
